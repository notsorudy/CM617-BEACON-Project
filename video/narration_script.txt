[1/10 title]
A state-of-health-aware framework for synthetic inertia delivery in solar and wind integrated grids. Course project for C M six seventeen, I I T Bombay.

[2/10 problem]
When a power plant suddenly trips, grid frequency starts to fall. Heavy spinning generators normally slow that fall. This is called inertia. Solar panels and wind turbines connect through inverters, and have no inertia. Grid-forming batteries can provide synthetic inertia instead, but most studies assume the battery always delivers its full rated power. Real batteries age. Their resistance rises, and the power they can deliver falls.

[3/10 system]
So we ask: how does battery ageing limit synthetic inertia? We study a system powered only by solar, or only by wind. A one hundred megawatt plant is paired with a twenty-five megawatt battery, which is the only source of inertia. We use real hourly solar and wind data for twenty twenty-five at Kutch, Gujarat, and compare three chemistries: L F P, N M C, and N C A.

[4/10 chain]
Our framework is a chain of four steps. First, the real solar or wind data sets how hard the battery works. Second, Model one, a random forest trained on Sandia cycling data, turns this workload into state of health, year by year. Third, Model two, learned from Severson's cells, turns state of health into resistance, and so into the maximum power the battery can still deliver. Fourth, in every hour of the year, we apply a sudden ten megawatt loss, and check whether the aged battery can hold the frequency.

[5/10 models]
Model one uses chemistry, temperature, depth of discharge, discharge rate, and cycle count. On cells it never saw, it reaches an R squared of zero point eight seven. For Model two, the common straight-line formula reaches zero point seven four. Our quadratic curve reaches zero point seven eight, almost as good as a random forest. At a state of health of zero point eight two, the battery keeps only eighty-six percent of its fresh power.

[6/10 fig_a]
Here is what ageing does over fifteen years. L F P ages slowly, and still delivers twenty-four point three megawatts at year ten. N M C and N C A drop to about twenty-one and a half. The dotted line is what the battery needs: twelve and a half megawatts for normal work, plus ten for the trip. N C A falls below it in year three, and N M C in year five.

[7/10 fig_b]
What happens to frequency? We simulated the first twenty seconds after a trip. Within its power limit, an aged battery behaves exactly like a new one. Frequency dips to forty-nine point eight hertz, and recovers. But once the aged battery hits its limit, it loses control. Frequency crosses forty-nine point four hertz, where load shedding begins, in under one second, and does not recover.

[8/10 fig_c]
How often does this happen? L F P never fails in fifteen years. N M C and N C A fail in about half a percent of hours with solar, and over one percent with wind. Wind is about twice as exposed, because it produces in twice as many hours. Think of the battery as a wallet with an emergency fund. As it ages, the wallet shrinks, and if the operator keeps spending as before, ageing eats the emergency fund. Our state-of-health-aware operation cuts everyday use just enough to protect the reserve. This removes every failure, at almost no cost.

[9/10 conclusion]
To conclude. An ageing battery gives full synthetic inertia, until its power ceiling falls below what it is asked to carry. L F P needs about five percent extra power rating, N M C and N C A fifteen to seventeen percent. Tracking state of health and protecting the reserve solves the problem at almost no cost. Next steps are resistance data for each chemistry, grid-scale cells, and real-time control. Thank you.
